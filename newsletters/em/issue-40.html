BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT — 26 NOVEMBER 2026

ACT ON THIS NOW

No practice change this week. The action points are checks and confirmations.

SAFETY Toradol injection: leaflets in the affected batches (currently all available stock) omit the warning about recent or planned stomach or bowel surgery; ask patients directly before giving ketorolac (MHRA Class 4, 24 November).

REVIEW Anaphylaxis discharge: NICE NG258 (May 2026) replaced CG134: two in-date auto-injectors, risk-stratified observation, second tryptase 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset (aged 16 or over). A new BMJ Practice Pointer differs on all three.

WORTH KNOWING ON SHIFT

COHORT Capnography after intubation: no adequate trace in 4.3% of successful intubations, two-thirds because the sampling line was not connected. If there is no trace, check the line at once; if a sustained trace does not appear immediately, assume oesophageal intubation (no trace, wrong place).

COHORT DOACs and head injury (London): ICH on CT 7.1% vs 7.7% (aOR 0.93) among patients who were scanned; NICE NG232 CT advice stands.

COHORT Massive GHB: first GCS 3 in 75%, 66% intubated, 1 death in 154; opioid co-detection linked with hypotension (aOR 4.51).

DIAGNOSTIC Paracetamol risk score (Edinburgh): specificity 82.5% vs 62.6% for the ALT–paracetamol product at the same sensitivity; internal validation only.

KNOW FOR NEXT TIME

RCT Prehospital fluids (PITSTOP): about 910 ml more saline before handover, mortality 18.0% vs 18.3%, pulmonary oedema in transport 2.2% vs 1.0%.

RCT Ambulance ceftriaxone (PITSTOP): mortality 18.0% vs 18.4% with antibiotics 3 to 4 hours earlier; drug-related adverse events 6.2% vs 1.5%.

GUIDELINE ED sedation (ACEP-led Delphi): do not delay urgent procedures for fasting, use capnography; it departs from UK guidance on oxygen, ASA referral and oral fluids.

COHORT Traumatic pneumothorax: 68% left the ED without a drain; size over 35 mm the strongest predictor of an ED drain (reflects practice, not a validated cut-off).

DIAGNOSTIC Renal POCUS in pyelonephritis: hydronephrosis 84% sensitive, 85% specific for a stone (LR− 0.2); a normal scan does not rule out a stone in patients with previous stones.

PAEDS Agitation, 9 to 17 years: IM olanzapine not superior to droperidol (53% vs 62% settled without rescue); neither is the NICE NG10 choice for children.

PAEDS Febrile infants: a positive viral panel lowers but does not remove SBI risk (6.5% vs 35.8%; 5.5% still had a UTI).

Sepsis fluids take the week. FISSH asks whether the crystalloid matters in septic shock and finds no large difference between Ringer’s lactate and saline. The two PITSTOP trials go earlier still, to the ambulance: neither more fluid nor earlier ceftriaxone showed a mortality difference, though both confidence intervals still allow a small effect. RCT

Ringer’s Lactate vs 0.9% Saline in Septic Shock (FISSH, Blinded RCT, 30 Hospitals, n=1,103): 30-Day In-Hospital Mortality 33.6% vs 33.0%, RR 1.02 (0.86 to 1.21), in a Trial Powered for an 8-Point Difference

Rochwerg B, Lamontagne F, Austin P, et al; Canadian Critical Care Trials Group. Ringer’s lactate versus saline in the fluids in septic shock (FISSH) trial: a multicentre, blinded, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial. Lancet Respir Med. 2026 (online ahead of print, 24 November). doi:10.1016/S2213-2600(26)00347-4. PMID: 43078115. NCT03677102. Source route: full text via the BMA Library (ClinicalKey, read 26 November); no expert review found. Background: BEST-Living IPD meta-analysis (6 ICU trials, 34,685 patients), balanced vs saline absolute difference in death −0.4 points (95% CrI −1.5 to 0.2), Lancet Respir Med 2024;12(3):237–246, doi:10.1016/S2213-2600(23)00417-4. Appraised from full text

Blinded trial at 30 hospitals in Canada and Saudi Arabia (2018 to 2026) in adults with suspected infection and SBP below 90 mmHg or MAP below 65 mmHg after a 1 L bolus, or lactate above 4 mmol/L, randomised within 6 hours of ED presentation or ICU consultation (62% from the ED); 88% were on vasoactive drugs and mean lactate was 4.8 mmol/L. All bolus and maintenance fluid until ICU discharge or day 30 was Ringer’s lactate or 0.9% saline in covered bags (mean 5,990 vs 5,695 mL). Death by day 30 in hospital occurred in 187 of 557 vs 180 of 546 (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.86 to 1.21; p=0.80); ICU mortality, AKI (40.8% vs 39.6%) and free-day outcomes did not differ. Ringer’s lactate reduced hyperchloraemia (chloride above 110 mmol/L: 221/524 [42.2%] vs 292/513 [56.9%]; RR 0.74, 0.65 to 0.84), hypernatraemia and metabolic acidosis without more hyperkalaemia. The trial was powered for an 8-point mortality reduction, about 20 times the BEST-Living estimate, so a small difference cannot be excluded.

INFORMING PRACTICE RCT

Liberal vs Usual Prehospital Saline in Suspected Sepsis (PITSTOP-Fluids, Factorial RCT, n=1,646): About 910 ml More Before Handover, Hospital Mortality 18.0% vs 18.3%, Pulmonary Oedema in Transport 2.2% vs 1.0%

Scales DC, Rogowsky A, Seymour CW, et al; PITSTOP Investigators. Prehospital fluid resuscitation for patients with suspected sepsis: the PITSTOP-Fluids factorial randomized clinical trial. JAMA. 2026 (online ahead of print, 24 November). doi:10.1001/jama.2026.25614. PMID: 43076208. NCT03068741. Source route: full text read (JAMA PDF via the BMA Library); no expert review found. Companion paper: PITSTOP-Antibiotics (next item). Appraised from full text

Factorial trial in four Ontario paramedic services (March 2020 to August 2025) in adults with paramedic-suspected infection, temperature 38.0°C or more and SBP below 100 mmHg who had IV access (405 randomised without access were excluded); median age 76, 16.4% vs 18.5% long-term care residents. Liberal care meant up to 2 L of 0.9% saline regardless of later SBP, by pump or pressure bag; usual care meant saline only while SBP was below 90 mmHg, stopped at 100. The liberal arm received median 1,500 vs 500 ml before handover (difference 910 ml, 95% CI 857 to 963), about 20 ml/kg for a 70 kg patient (the authors’ estimate; weights not reported); 85.3% of the usual arm still had a bolus. Hospital mortality was 149 of 830 vs 149 of 816 (RR 0.98, 0.80 to 1.20; risk difference −0.3 points, −4.0 to 3.4) and 90-day mortality 26.6% vs 26.0%. Fewer liberal-arm patients were hypotensive at ED arrival (45.6% vs 54.4%), ED teams then gave similar volumes (median 1,225 vs 1,500 ml in 24 hours), and the 24-hour total was median 3,000 vs 2,030 ml; pulmonary oedema in transport was 19 of 848 vs 8 of 821, and the lower ICD-coded AKI with liberal fluids (31.4% vs 39.1%) was a post hoc outcome.

UK practice: NICE NG253, suspected sepsis in people aged 16 or over (2025, updated September 2026) recs 1.8.4 to 1.8.9 ask for an IV bolus within 1 hour for high risk, a balanced crystalloid such as Hartmann’s (0.9% saline if none is available), 250 ml over 10 to 15 minutes, reassessment after each bolus, further boluses up to 1,000 ml total including any fluids previously given, and senior advice if not improved after 1,000 ml. For the ED: a patient who arrives after 1 to 2 L of ambulance saline was not harmed or helped on mortality in this trial; count those fluids towards the 1,000 ml in rec 1.8.7 and reassess before more, listening for pulmonary oedema. What it does not show: anything about how much fluid the ED itself should give, balanced fluid, patients still in shock after resuscitation, afebrile sepsis, or 30 ml/kg, which the liberal arm did not reach.

Intramuscular Ceftriaxone vs Placebo in the Ambulance for Suspected Sepsis (PITSTOP-Antibiotics, Blinded Factorial RCT, n=2,048): Hospital Mortality 18.0% vs 18.4% With Antibiotics Given 3 to 4 Hours Earlier

Same trial as PITSTOP-Fluids (previous item). Appraised from full text

The antibiotic factor of the same Ontario trial, in all 2,048 eligible patients with or without IV access: 1 g of IM ceftriaxone or 2.8 ml of IM saline from identical vials, with patients, paramedics, ED clinicians and analysts blinded and open-label ED antibiotics allowed; NEWS2 was 7 or more in 77.8% vs 77.2%, and patients known to take anticoagulants or to be allergic to penicillin or cephalosporins were excluded. DOACs and Acute Intracranial Haemorrhage After Head Injury in Three London EDs (Retrospective Cohort, 11,401 CT Episodes): ICH 7.1% With a DOAC vs 7.7% Without, Adjusted OR 0.93 (0.70 to 1.21), in Patients Who Were Scanned

Bloom B, Pott J, Skene I, et al. Direct oral anticoagulant use and acute intracranial hemorrhage in adults undergoing CT after head injury. JAMA Netw Open. 2026;9(11):e2645624. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.45624. PMID: 43071377. Source route: PubMed sweep; open-access full text from PMC (PMC13794055); no expert review found by 26 November 2026. Appraised from full text

Retrospective cohort (2018 to 2022) of adults with a coded chief concern of head injury who had a head CT at three Barts Health EDs, with DOAC exposure taken from linked primary-care prescribing records (active prescription at presentation) and compared with no anticoagulant; 28% of 16,050 eligible episodes could not be linked and were excluded, as were vitamin K antagonist and heparin users. DOAC episodes (565) had a median age of 84 vs 50 years in the 10,836 without anticoagulation, and more frailty. Acute intracranial haemorrhage on the first CT occurred in 40 of 565 (7.1%) vs 831 of 10,836 (7.7%); adjusted OR 0.93 (95% CI 0.70 to 1.21). Neurosurgery was rare (2 vs 44 episodes, 0.4% in each group; adjusted OR 1.58, 0.47 to 4.17), and crude 28-day mortality was 3.0% vs 1.7% (adjusted OR 0.70, 0.46 to 1.01), a reversal the authors attribute to confounding by age and frailty, not protection. The primary adjusted model used complete cases, 6,480 of 11,401 episodes (56.8%). Because NICE advises considering CT for anticoagulated people with no other indication, DOAC patients were probably scanned after milder injuries than those without, which dilutes their haemorrhage rate.

Waveform Capnography Failure After Successful ED Intubation (US Single-Centre Cohort, 2,875 Intubations): 4.3% Had No Adequate Trace Despite a Tracheal Tube, Two-Thirds Because the Sampling Line Was Not Connected; One Tube Was Removed

Driver BE, Robinson AE, Kuo J, Prekker ME. Frequency and causes of waveform capnography failure after successful emergency tracheal intubation. Ann Emerg Med. 2026 (online ahead of print, 23 November). doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2026.09.025. PMID: 43076805. Source route: PubMed sweep; full text via the BMA Library (ClinicalKey), read 25 November; no expert review found. Appraised from full text

Retrospective analysis (January 2022 to April 2025) of a prospectively collected quality-improvement database at one urban US safety-net ED, where trained observers recorded intubations in real time and the intubating doctor (mostly a third-year EM resident; video laryngoscopy in 93%) completed a structured form. Of 2,875 successful tracheal intubations in patients aged 14 or over, 124 (4.3%, 95% CI 3.6 to 5.1) had a CO2 waveform problem, absent or abnormal, with the tube in the trachea. The sampling line was not connected to the bag or monitor in 82 (66% of failures, about 1 in 35 intubations), equipment failed in 11 (9%), mostly the CO2 module, and 30 (24%) were never explained; categories could overlap. During CPR, unexplained failure occurred in 6 of 173 intubations (3.5%) vs 24 of 2,702 (0.9%) without CPR. One tube, in a patient in cardiac arrest, was removed because of the absent waveform; no trace appeared through a supraglottic airway either, and the trace returned after the module was replaced and the patient reintubated. Failure was self-reported without formal waveform criteria, and tracheal placement was judged by the treating doctor.
