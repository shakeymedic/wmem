BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT — NOVEMBER 2026

ACT ON THIS NOW

SAFETY CRRT and echinocandins: PAN membranes can adsorb caspofungin, most of all on a new filter. Where possible, give echinocandins at least 6 hours after CRRT is initiated or a routine filter circuit set change; do not delay or pause CRRT solely to give the drug; for first or loading doses, which the alert does not address, seek microbiology advice.

WORTH KNOWING ON SHIFT

RCT Renal replacement (ICRAKI): keep starting with CRRT in ventilated or vasopressor-dependent AKI; intermittent haemodialysis first was not shown to be non-inferior, and crude mortality was higher (secondary outcome, trial stopped early).

OBS Tumour lysis syndrome: rasburicase within 12 hours was associated with less AKI needing KRT or death (32.7% vs 42.0%; adjusted OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.88; 1,276 US adults).

RCT Dobutamine in septic shock surgery: added to noradrenaline during source control, it cut new or worsening AKI from 34.7% to 22.8% (adjusted RR 0.64, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.83), with no significant effect on mortality.

OBS Malignant hyperthermia (UK): modelled incidence 7.8 per million general anaesthetics; previous uneventful anaesthetics do not exclude MH.

PAEDS Clear fluid fasting: aspiration was 0.03% under both liberal and conventional policies across 580,197 children (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.40 to 1.47; very low certainty).

KNOW FOR NEXT TIME

RCT Isoflurane sedation (SAVE-ICU): no lower hospital mortality than intravenous sedation (42.5% vs 43.8%; RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.84 to 1.14), with fewer delirium- and coma-free days.

SR/MA Daily sedation interruption (Cochrane): probably reduces mortality versus no interruption (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.99); no benefit shown over protocolised light sedation.

RCT PELICAN: cardiac index-guided fluid and dobutamine in elective major abdominal surgery did not reduce 7-day complications (44.3% vs 46.9%).

RCT High-flow nasal oxygen after VATS: lower odds of a worse pulmonary complication grade (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.91) in 60- to 80-year-olds, driven by grade 2 events, with no change in length of stay.

SR/MA Prehabilitation: programmes combining aerobic, pulmonary and functional exercise ranked best for complications, at very low certainty.

OBS Caesarean GA (Europe, n=9,989): 14.8% had GA, almost half after conversion from neuraxial anaesthesia.

OBS Videolaryngoscopy: tube delivery was difficult or failed in 6.3% of intubations despite a good glottic view; record how the tube went in, not only the view.

OBS CT angiography for death by neurological criteria: circulatory arrest was shown in 67.4% of scans within 12 hours of brainstem areflexia vs 98.7% after 12 hours (n=118).

SR/MA ARDS endotypes: 4 of 11 secondary analyses of RCTs found a significant treatment-endotype interaction, but only one, liberal fluids in hyperinflammatory ARDS (90-day mortality 40% vs 50%), was rated high credibility.

OBS ARDS subphenotypes (PHIND, UK): a 1-hour near-patient assay classed 18% of 490 patients as hyperinflammatory, with 60-day mortality of 51% vs 28% (RR 1.8, 95% CI 1.4 to 2.4); not yet shown to guide treatment.

Intermittent Haemodialysis vs CRRT as the First Modality in Shocked or Ventilated AKI (ICRAKI, French RCT, 554 Randomised, 535 Analysed): Non-Inferiority Not Shown; Crude 90-Day Mortality, a Secondary Outcome, Was 63.1% vs 53.5% (HR 1.30) in a Trial Stopped Early

Gaudry S, Boubaya M, Louis G, et al; ICRAKI Trial Group. Intermittent or continuous renal-replacement therapy in acute kidney injury. N Engl J Med. 2026;395:1677–1690. doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2608397. PMID: 42873070. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06032884. Source route: HAL open-archive copy (hal-05800989, CC BY 4.0), the authors’ manuscript with NEJM-formatted tables and figures, not the publisher-typeset version. Appraised from full text

In 38 French ICUs, adults with AKI and a recognised indication for renal-replacement therapy who were receiving, or had received, invasive ventilation or catecholamines were randomised to intermittent haemodialysis (IHD) or CRRT as the initial modality for days 0 to 3. The primary outcome was a major adverse kidney event by day 90 (death, RRT dependence or creatinine more than 25% above baseline), with a 7.5-point non-inferiority margin for IHD. Enrolment stopped on the monitoring board’s advice at 554 of a planned 1,000 patients because of a survival difference favouring CRRT; in the 535-patient ITT population the primary outcome occurred in 78.1% vs 71.6% (adjusted ARD 6.28 points, 95% CI −1.22 to 13.79), so non-inferiority was not shown and CRRT was not significantly superior. Crude death by day 90, a secondary outcome, occurred in 63.1% vs 53.5% (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.63), while hypotension was similar (68.8% vs 66.5%).

UK practice: NICE NG148, Acute kidney injury (2019, updated October 2024) covers when to start renal-replacement therapy and whom to involve but makes no recommendation on modality. Rasburicase Within 12 Hours of Tumour Lysis Syndrome (STOP-TLS Emulated Target Trial, 36 US Hospitals, n=1,276): AKI Needing Kidney Replacement Therapy or Death 32.7% vs 42.0% (Adjusted OR 0.67)

Shenoy T, Sanchez-Almanzar D, Dias JA, et al. Early treatment with rasburicase and risk of kidney replacement therapy and death in adults with tumour lysis syndrome: emulated target trial. BMJ. 2026;394:e100040. doi:10.1136/bmj-2026-100040. PMID: 42492944. No registration reported. Source route: publisher PDF supplied by Jake (bmj.com). Appraised from full text

Retrospective multicentre cohort (2014–2023) of 1,276 adults with laboratory and clinical tumour lysis syndrome (TLS) and a high tumour burden, analysed as an emulated trial of rasburicase within 12 hours of TLS onset (705 patients, given at a median 5.0 hours) versus delayed or no rasburicase (571, of whom 193 received it after 12 hours). Patients with recent rasburicase, a life-threatening indication for kidney replacement therapy (KRT) at onset, a moribund state or known G6PD deficiency were excluded; most had a haematological malignancy and about one in six were in ICU at onset. After inverse probability weighting, the primary outcome of AKI needing KRT or death in hospital occurred in 32.7% vs 42.0% (adjusted OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.88), and 90-day mortality was 45.6% vs 54.3% (adjusted OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.94). Three treated patients (0.4%) had a rasburicase-related adverse event.

  • 11.2% vs 17.0% AKI NEEDING KRT ALONE, AOR 0.62 (0.44 TO 0.87)

UK practice: The Fasturtec SmPC (eMC, revised January 2023) licenses rasburicase for treatment and prophylaxis of acute hyperuricaemia in haematological malignancy with a high tumour burden at the start of chemotherapy, at 0.20 mg/kg/day, and contraindicates it in G6PD deficiency; this cohort mostly used fixed 3 or 6 mg doses and included solid tumours. The NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service (updated September 2025) states that the updated BSH tumour lysis syndrome guideline (2025) recommends rasburicase be available for administration within 1 hour of prescription.

Isoflurane-Based Inhaled Sedation in Ventilated Adults With Hypoxaemic Respiratory Failure (SAVE-ICU, n=786 Randomised): No Reduction in Hospital Mortality (42.5% vs 43.8%)

Jerath A, Slessarev M, Martin C, et al. Isoflurane-Based Inhaled Sedation in Mechanically Ventilated Adults With Acute Hypoxemic Respiratory Failure: The SAVE-ICU Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. 2026 (published online 14 October 2026). doi:10.1001/jama.2026.18471. PMID: 42872958. NCT04415060. Source route: PDF via BMA Library; supplements not read. Appraised from full text

Pragmatic, open-label superiority RCT in 14 Canadian ICUs (September 2020 to March 2025): adults on intravenous sedation for less than 72 hours and ventilated for acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure (PaO2:FiO2 below 300 mm Hg [40 kPa], or COVID-19) were randomised to an isoflurane-based inhaled strategy (sevoflurane at 2 sites) with intravenous adjuncts as needed, or to usual intravenous sedation. Of 786 randomised, 780 formed the primary analysis population (6 randomised in error) and 769 had the primary outcome (11 withdrew consent). All-cause hospital mortality did not differ: 161/379 (42.5%) vs 171/390 (43.8%), RR 0.98 (95% CI 0.84 to 1.14), absolute difference −1.37 points (−8.37 to 5.64). Only 88.5% of the inhaled group received the intervention, and 49.9% to 55.4% also received intravenous sedation on any day in the first 14 days. The inhaled group received more refractory hypoxaemia adjuncts (63.9% vs 55.0%; difference 8.89 points, 1.95 to 15.83), had fewer delirium- and coma-free days (−0.95, −1.69 to −0.22) and had all 5 serious adverse events (raised intracranial pressure in 2, prolonged asystole, propofol infusion syndrome, and cardiovascular instability with seizure) and all 5 abnormal liver function adverse events (5/385 vs 0/395).

Daily Sedation Interruption vs No Interruption (Cochrane Update, 23 RCTs, n=5,987): Mortality RR 0.88, About 1 Fewer Death per 35 Adults; Moderate Certainty by the Authors’ GRADE, Low by Ours

Rose L, Williamson DR, McCullagh IJ, et al. Daily sedation interruption versus no daily sedation interruption for critically ill adults and children requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2026;10:CD009176. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD009176.pub3. PMID: 42841340. Registration CD009176 (protocol 2011). Source route: Cochrane Library full text (PDF); supplementary analyses not read. Appraised from full text

Cochrane review update (search to 8 October 2025) of 23 RCTs (18 adult, 5 paediatric; 5,987 participants, 4,910 after cluster adjustment) comparing daily sedation interruption (DSI) with protocolised sedation (9 trials), usual clinician-directed sedation (9) or analgesia-first or no-sedation strategies (5). DSI probably reduced mortality (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.99; I² 0%; 18 trials, 4,057 participants; moderate certainty), from 245 to 216 per 1,000 adults. It may shorten ventilation by 14% (1% to 25%; about 1 day in adults; I² 88%; low certainty) and probably shortened ICU stay by 11% (2% to 20%) and reduced tracheostomy (RR 0.68, 0.55 to 0.85). Accidental extubation did not clearly differ (RR 0.88, 0.66 to 1.19; low certainty), and DSI probably increased total morphine dose by 7% (0% to 14%).

Timing of CT Angiography for Death by Neurological Criteria (Polish Cohort, n=118): Circulatory Arrest Confirmed in 67.4% When Scanned Within 12 h of Brainstem Areflexia vs 98.7% After 12 h

Bohatyrewicz R, Sawicki M, Chamier-Ciemińska K, et al. Optimum timing of cerebral computed tomography angiography for determination of death using neurological criteria: a retrospective sensitivity cohort study. Anaesthesia. 2026 (published online 21 October 2026). doi:10.1111/anae.70438. PMID: 42906612. Source route: open-access full text (publisher PDF, CC BY-NC); supporting appendices S1 and S2 not read. Appraised from full text

Single-centre retrospective cohort from Szczecin, Poland (August 2012 to May 2022): 118 ICU patients with devastating brain injury and initial signs of brainstem areflexia (fixed dilated pupils, loss of cough and gag), all of whom eventually had cerebral circulatory arrest confirmed. The first CTA, read against the Polish four-point criteria, confirmed arrest in 103/118 (87.3%, 95% CI 79.6% to 92.5%), and 14 of the 15 scans showing flow were done within 12 hours of areflexia onset. Confirmation rose from 29/43 (67.4%, 51.3% to 80.5%) at 12 hours or less to 74/75 (98.7%, 91.8% to 99.9%) after 12 hours, and all 15 repeat scans confirmed arrest. The authors recommend waiting at least 12 hours, and repeating an inconclusive CTA at least 12 hours later.

  • 74/75 (98.7%) CONFIRMED AFTER 12 H (95% CI 91.8 TO 99.9)
  • 4/7 (57.1%) CONFIRMED WHEN SCANNED UNDER 6 H (95% CI 20.2 TO 88.2)
  • n=118 ALL WITH ARREST EVENTUALLY CONFIRMED, SO SPECIFICITY CANNOT BE ESTIMATED

Why Syndrome RCTs Keep Failing: The Fruit-Peeler Problem, and What the Evidence Actually Shows

A methods feature built around a critical care blog post. Opinion, the blog’s and ours, is labelled as such; every trial number below is from the primary source.

The argument. FOAMED In “The Fruit-Peeler RCT” (The Thoughtful Intensivist, Substack, 15 July 2026), Rafael Olivé Leite argues that sepsis and ARDS criteria are cause-agnostic. His analogy, credited to a post by “Dr Lynn” on X (we take this to be Lawrence Lynn, author of the review below), is a trial that enrols anything that is “a fruit” and “round” and peels them all: the apple looks good, the orange is harmed, and “depending on the case mix, the average effect may be beneficial, neutral, or harmful.” From this he concludes that such results lack transportability, that “all sepsis and ARDS RCTs ever performed are fruit-peeler RCTs”, and that “the field would never find a beneficial treatment for sepsis and ARDS, and the prediction holds.” He also criticises the PROVE Network for “15 RCTs in 15 years”. Our view: this is a polemic by a named author, not a study, and its core point about case mix is reasonable. Its strongest claim, that syndrome trials never find benefit, is the one to test. The formal version. Lynn’s single-author review in Patient Safety in Surgery (2026; PMID: 42464342; PMC full text) sets out the same idea mathematically. A trial gated on syndrome criteria estimates a “mean of causally unrelated means”: an average of disease-specific effects weighted by whatever mix of diseases a trial happens to enrol. If that mix shifts between trials, the same treatment can appear to help, do nothing or harm (his “cause-mixture paradox”), which he distinguishes from Simpson’s paradox because statistical adjustment alone cannot resolve it. Two points matter for readers. First, this is not independent support for the blog: Lynn is the source of the analogy, the blog author writes that he has argued it for a few years and Lynn for many more, and the review contains no new patient data (Lynn declares that he owns a company developing medical-record and time-series software). Second, Lynn is less absolute than the blog: he writes that the framework “does not suggest that all cause-agnostic trials are invalid”, and that pooling may be justified when different diseases truly converge on a shared physiological pathway that the intervention can modify. What the evidence shows: heterogeneity is real but modest. If the peeler problem were the whole story, splitting patients by biology should reveal large, consistent opposite effects. Four recent papers test that.

These trials do not settle the argument entirely. Lynn himself cites lung-protective ventilation (ARMA) as an example of his “physiologic funnel” exception, where different causes share one vulnerability; PROSEVA’s physiological gate (severe ARDS only) arguably fits it too, though that is our reading. ARMA was also stopped early, which tends to overstate benefit. The steroid trials agree that shock resolves faster but disagree on mortality in different populations and regimens, which is the kind of non-transportable average the blog describes. Our view: syndrome trials can find real benefit when the intervention acts on a shared physiological problem, and are more likely to fail when it targets one pathway among many.

Bottom line (our view): be sceptical of both the trial average and the post hoc subgroup. Low tidal volume ventilation in all ARDS and prone positioning in moderate or severe ARDS remain established, and the FICM/ICS ARDS guideline still recommends both. Watch for prospective subphenotype-stratified trials such as PANTHER (ISRCTN81435672, recruiting in England since November 2025) before changing practice for any subphenotype.

The table compares the Association of Anaesthetists (2020) and European Malignant Hyperthermia Group (EMHG, 2024) guidelines.

1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND HOW COMMON IT IS

MH susceptibility is classically described as autosomal dominant (each first-degree relative has a 50% chance of carrying the variant), although a 2026 UK analysis found that the clinical risk of reacting fits a threshold model better. The genes implicated (EMHG) are RYR1 (the ryanodine receptor, the calcium release channel of the sarcoplasmic reticulum), CACNA1S (the dihydropyridine receptor, DHPR) and STAC3 (which links RYR1 and the DHPR). In a susceptible patient, a volatile agent or suxamethonium releases excess calcium into the muscle cell. Energy-dependent ATPases work to clear it, driving up oxygen consumption, CO2 production, heat and heart rate. When calcium homeostasis fails, the muscle contracts, leaks potassium and then myoglobin and creatine kinase (CK), and the acidosis becomes mixed. Dantrolene binds RYR1 and suppresses calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. The Association of Anaesthetists 2020 guideline puts UK incidence at about 1 in 100,000 general anaesthetics (about 20 confirmed cases a year against about 3 million anaesthetics from NAP5 and NAP6), or about 1 in 250,000 if one anaesthetic per 10 people a year is used as the denominator; UK mortality is about 4%. The 2026 UK modelling paper in this issue estimates 7.8 reactions per million general anaesthetics (1 in 128,000) for 1988–93, and a prevalence of clinical MH risk of 1 in 38,800 (95% credibility interval 1 in 34,600 to 1 in 43,700).

2. TRIGGERS, SAFE AGENTS AND PATIENTS AT RISK

Triggers are all volatile anaesthetic agents and suxamethonium. Safe options are regional anaesthesia or total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA). Prepare the machine by flushing with 100% oxygen to below 5 ppm of volatile, or fit activated charcoal filters to both limbs; every UK hospital giving general anaesthesia should have these filters. Treat as at increased risk (Association of Anaesthetists, Box 5): confirmed MH susceptibility (a pathogenic variant or a positive in vitro contracture test, IVCT); blood relatives of a confirmed case; a suggestive personal or family anaesthetic history; a myopathy caused by a gene implicated in MH (RYR1, CACNA1S, STAC3, as named by EMHG); a variant of unknown significance in those genes; unexplained or recurrent rhabdomyolysis; idiopathic raised CK; and unexplained exertional heat illness. EMHG 2026 adds that patients with an RYR1-related myopathy, a STAC3 myopathy or a CACNA1S/CACNB1 myopathy should not receive volatiles unless their own IVCT is MH-negative. Suxamethonium is contraindicated in congenital myopathies; use rocuronium for rapid sequence induction and keep sugammadex available. Prophylactic dantrolene is not recommended, because it worsens weakness. In muscular dystrophies the concern is perioperative rhabdomyolysis: volatiles may be used at the lowest dose for the shortest time, but TIVA is preferred.

3. RECOGNITION: EARLY AND LATE SIGNS

No single sign is diagnostic; the pattern matters. The cardinal triad (Association of Anaesthetists, Box 1) is an unexplained, unexpected rise in end-tidal CO2, heart rate and temperature. Early signs (EMHG 2024) are raised CO2 production or tachypnoea, increased O2 consumption, mixed acidosis, sweating and mottling; tachycardia, ventricular ectopics or bigeminy and unstable blood pressure; and masseter spasm after suxamethonium or generalised rigidity. Late signs are hyperkalaemia, a rapid rise in core temperature, grossly raised CK and myoglobin, dark urine, severe arrhythmias or arrest, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Timing: onset can be within 10 min of exposure or hours later, especially with desflurane. A temperature rise that starts before any rise in CO2 production is not MH. Differentials (EMHG 2024) include light anaesthesia, sepsis, inadequate ventilation, machine fault, anaphylaxis, phaeochromocytoma, thyroid crisis, laparoscopic CO2, ecstasy, neuroleptic malignant syndrome and serotonin syndrome (new in 2024). In an undiagnosed myopathy, a rapidly rising end-tidal CO2 and mixed acidosis point to MH. Sudden arrest or cola-coloured urine points to rhabdomyolysis, where the priority is cardiac output while lowering potassium.

4. MANAGING THE CRISIS

Declare an emergency and call for help. Three things happen together: eliminate the trigger, give IV dantrolene and start active cooling. EMHG advises asking the surgeon to stop or postpone surgery. The UK guideline allows surgery to be completed under IV anaesthesia once the reaction is controlled and its consequences need no further management.

In a UK theatre (Association of Anaesthetists 2020): give dantrolene 2–3 mg kg−1 on actual body weight now, giving each syringe as soon as it is made up; then 1 mg kg−1 every 5 min until end-tidal CO2 is below 6 kPa with normal minute ventilation and core temperature is below 38.5 °C; formally reappraise the diagnosis at a cumulative 10 mg kg−1, but continue if MH is still likely. The guideline sets no per-dose cap.

5. AFTER THE CRISIS

Before discharge, give the patient written information and ask them to warn all blood relatives. Point them to the UK MH Registry (ukmhr.ac.uk). The consultant anaesthetist in charge must refer directly to the MH Investigation Unit in Leeds, which serves the UK and Ireland, sending the perioperative records and a narrative account. Testing is nationally commissioned in England; elsewhere it needs funding approval. Testing: DNA screening on a blood sample taken by the GP usually comes first, but it detects only about 50% of susceptibility. If no variant is found, patients aged over 10 years have an IVCT on freshly excised muscle at Leeds. For an index case under 10, both parents are offered biopsy. Biopsy is not done within 4 months of a reaction, and the result is given on the day. Those at risk are advised to wear a MedicAlert or similar tag and given warning cards. The EMHG 2025 investigation guideline adds a new “MHG” (MH genotype) designation alongside “MHS” (MH susceptible). It also defines clinical MH as limited to the classic triggers.
