Lead — decompressive craniectomy: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (n=1,326) found that decompressive craniectomy did not reduce unfavourable outcome in adults with moderate to severe TBI (61.5% vs 60.2%; RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.20). Any mortality gain from last-tier craniectomy may come as survival with severe disability, so the decision belongs in a frank conversation with the family. BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT — NOVEMBER 2026

WORTH KNOWING ON SHIFT

OBS TXA: in two pooled prehospital trials (n=1,249), 30-day death was highest with TEG shutdown (14.5%), where TXA was linked to a lower hazard of death (HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.91) in a subgroup likely defined after the prehospital dose; do not withhold TXA on an arrival TEG or ROTEM.

OBS Tourniquet conversion: 83.9% of 647 civilian prehospital tourniquets were converted in the ED without a procedure or operation, and each extra 30 minutes was associated with lower odds of success (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.53); reassess every tourniquet on arrival.

OBS Burn size at referral: overestimated in 76% of 82 patients sent to a Swedish burn centre (median 10% vs 6% TBSA); recalculate with Lund and Browder before fixing fluids or the level of burn service.

OBS Subdural haematoma with GCS 13–15: 10% of those aged 65 or over died or were discharged to hospice vs 3% under 65 (n=1,157).

DX Shock index: a weak predictor of trauma death on its own (LR+ 2.86, LR− 0.42); a positive result lifts a 20% prior only to 42%, so use it as a flag, not a decision.

UK Blood supply: O and B D-negative red cells remain at Pre-Amber; O D-negative stock was 2.9 days on 5 November against a 6-day aim.

KNOW FOR NEXT TIME

SR/MA Decompressive craniectomy (Lead): no reduction in unfavourable outcome across 8 RCTs (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.20).

RCT Ankle fracture weight bearing: 2 weeks after fixation was non-inferior to 6 weeks for reoperation (2.3% vs 3.4%; n=418) with no significant difference in return to work (HR 1.1, 95% CI 0.8 to 1.6).

OBS TFNA nails: no significant difference in any reoperation vs PFNA (17.2% vs 12.9%, p=0.194), but more major revisions (7.8% vs 3.2%) in one cohort and more mechanical failures (1.6% vs 0.3%) in another.

OBS Platelets and respiratory failure: each platelet unit in 24 hours was linked to 7% higher odds of respiratory failure by day 7 (OR 1.071, 95% CI 1.021 to 1.122) in a post hoc analysis likely confounded by bleeding severity; keep NG39 and local major haemorrhage protocols.

OBS Mass casualty incidents: severely injured patients took longer to reach hospital (median 85 vs 69 minutes) without a clear difference in in-hospital death (adjusted OR 1.19, 95% CI 0.77 to 1.85).

PAEDS Telemedicine for injured children: adding video to the phone referral did not change parent experience, distress or transfer in a 10-ED stepped-wedge RCT, but only 44.7% actually had a video call.

REVIEW Pregnancy (Quick Hits): UK practice considers resuscitative hysterotomy from 20 weeks (fundus at the umbilicus) and gives at least 500 IU anti-D within 72 hours after abdominal trauma from 12 weeks, plus a Kleihauer test from 20 weeks.

Bouras M, Lamothe-Boucher F, Abou Jamous J, et al. Decompressive craniectomy in adults with moderate to severe traumatic brain injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. Crit Care Resusc. 2026;28(4):100220. doi:10.1016/j.ccrj.2026.100220. PMID: 42893097. PROSPERO CRD42024565322. Source route: PMC full text (PMC13676264; open access, CC BY-NC-ND); supplementary figures not read. Appraised from full text

Systematic review (search to 1 August 2026) of 8 RCTs (1,326 patients; at least 80% of each trial aged 18 or over) comparing decompressive craniectomy (DC) with medical management (4 trials, including DECRA and RESCUEicp) or with a surgical comparator (craniotomy in 3, including RESCUE-ASDH and PREDICT-AEDH; cisternostomy in 1); 5 trials tested primary DC, performed without regard to intracranial pressure, and 3 secondary DC for refractory intracranial hypertension. The primary outcome, unfavourable outcome (GOS 1–3 or GOS-E 1–4) at the latest follow-up (3 to 12 months), occurred in 397 of 646 (61.5%) with DC vs 391 of 650 (60.2%) controls (7 trials; RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.20; I² 68%; low certainty). Mortality was 26.5% vs 33.7% (8 trials; RR 0.84, 0.59 to 1.19; I² 65%; low certainty); it was lower with DC against medical management (RR 0.59, 0.43 to 0.81; 4 trials) and with secondary DC (RR 0.61, 0.42 to 0.87; 3 trials), but no subgroup showed a functional benefit. Quality of life was reported by 2 trials and did not differ.

Shock Index, RTS and ISS for Predicting Trauma Death (Diagnostic Meta-Analysis, 12 Studies, n=234,129): Shock Index LR+ 2.86 and LR− 0.42, So a Positive Result Raises a 20% Prior Only to 42%

Raza AA, Ahmad M, Choudhary N, et al. Diagnostic accuracy of shock index, revised trauma score, and injury severity score for mortality prediction in trauma: a hierarchical meta-analysis. Injury. 2026;57(11):113760. doi:10.1016/j.injury.2026.113760. PMID: 42854533. No protocol registration reported. Source route: PDF via BMA Library; supplementary files not read. Appraised from full text

Hierarchical (bivariate random-effects) meta-analysis of 12 mainly retrospective studies published 2014–2025 (search to May 2026; 234,129 adults from emergency department, trauma centre, registry and ICU cohorts in Türkiye, Slovenia, India, the USA, Iran, Qatar, South Africa and Pakistan), testing shock index (SI, 5 studies), Revised Trauma Score (RTS, 6) and Injury Severity Score (ISS, 9) against death; in-hospital mortality was the prespecified endpoint, and some studies used 7-, 28- or 30-day mortality. Pooled SI sensitivity was 0.679 (95% CI 0.544 to 0.789) and specificity 0.763 (0.446 to 0.928; AUC 0.747) at cut-offs from 0.86 to 1.08; RTS gave 0.788 (0.454 to 0.943) and 0.801 (0.683 to 0.882; AUC 0.852), and ISS 0.788 (0.712 to 0.848) and 0.796 (0.709 to 0.862; AUC 0.855). In the 6 studies reporting both, RTS and ISS did not differ (likelihood-ratio test p=0.914; within-study sensitivity OR 1.59, 0.82 to 3.07; specificity OR 0.89, 0.55 to 1.45), and SI could not be compared directly with RTS. One US National Trauma Data Bank study of older patients supplied 217,190 of the 234,129 patients (92.8%, calculated), all in the SI analysis.

Western Trauma Association Traumatic Haemothorax Algorithm (12 Authors, Level V Consensus): Four Decision Inputs, but No Thresholds in the Abstract

Hynes AM, Brown CVR, Hartwell JL, et al. Western Trauma Association critical decisions in trauma: traumatic hemothorax. J Trauma Acute Care Surg. 2026 (online ahead of print). doi:10.1097/TA.0000000000005207. PMID: 42862903. Source route: PubMed abstract only (the BMA Library cannot supply this journal); no published commentary found (JournalFeed, SGEM, St Emlyn’s, REBEL EM, EMCrit, EAST/AAST journal club and journal commentary searched 3 Nov 2026). Abstract only

A Western Trauma Association consensus algorithm, by 12 authors from US centres, for traumatic haemothorax, whose management ranges from observation to tube thoracostomy and operation. The recommendations came from a review of the literature, including randomised and observational studies, supplemented by expert consensus where high-quality evidence is limited (Level V). The pathway uses four inputs: the patient’s physiology, the size of the haemothorax, whether pleural drainage is adequate, and evidence of ongoing haemorrhage. The abstract gives no size or drain-output thresholds, no timing for evacuation of retained haemothorax and no grading of individual recommendations, and the authors present it as support for, not a replacement of, clinical judgement and a template for local protocols.

TXA by Fibrinolytic Phenotype (Two Pooled Prehospital TXA Trials, n=1,249): 30-Day Death Highest With TEG “Shutdown” (14.5%), Where TXA Was Linked to a Lower Hazard of Death (HR 0.32) in a Subgroup Likely Defined After the Prehospital Dose (TEG Timing Not Reported in the Abstract)

Kenny JE, Brito AM, Benchiba-Charron M, et al. Survival benefit of prehospital TXA in fibrinolytic shutdown: insights from two prospective trauma trials. J Trauma Acute Care Surg. 2026 (online ahead of print). doi:10.1097/TA.0000000000005092. PMID: 42853261. Source route: PubMed abstract only (journal not supplied by the BMA Library); no published commentary found (PubMed comment links, Europe PMC, JournalFeed, EMA Daily, SGEM, St Emlyn’s, REBEL EM, EMCrit, EAST literature reviews and journal commentary searched 3 Nov 2026). Abstract only

Secondary analysis of a harmonised dataset from two prospective prehospital TXA trials (the Prehospital Tranexamic Acid Use for Traumatic Brain Injury trial and the STAAMP air and ground transport trial): 1,249 injured patients grouped by their initial thromboelastography (TEG) LY30, the clot-lysis measure on the TEG trace, into fibrinolytic shutdown (below 0.9%), physiological (0.9–3%) and hyperfibrinolysis (above 3%). The primary outcome, 30-day mortality, was 14.5% with shutdown, 6.7% with hyperfibrinolysis and 6.5% with physiological fibrinolysis. Adjusted for TXA, phenotype, age, mechanism, Injury Severity Score and shock index on hospital arrival, hyperfibrinolysis carried a higher hazard of death (HR 2.87, 95% CI 1.10 to 7.53; reference group not stated), and patients in shutdown randomised to TXA had a lower hazard of death (HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.91). Phenotype group sizes, TXA-versus-placebo event rates within each phenotype, when the TEG was drawn relative to the TXA dose, and adverse events are not reported in the abstract.

1. ISNCSCI: LEVELS AND THE AIS GRADE

The ISNCSCI worksheet (ASIA/ISCOS) sets the order. (1) Sensory level on each side: the most caudal intact dermatome for both pin prick and light touch. (2) Motor level on each side: the most caudal key muscle function graded at least 3 (on supine testing), provided the key muscle functions above it are intact (graded 5); where there is no myotome to test, the motor level is presumed to be the same as the sensory level if testable motor function above it is normal. (3) Neurological level of injury (NLI): the most cephalad of the sensory and motor levels. (4) Complete or incomplete: the injury is complete only if voluntary anal contraction (VAC) is absent, all S4–5 sensory scores are 0 and deep anal pressure (DAP) is absent. (5) AIS grade (table). (6) Zone of partial preservation, used only when sacral motor or sensory function is absent. In an apparent AIS B, test non-key muscles more than 3 segments below the motor level on each side to separate B from C or D.

2. SPINAL SHOCK VS NEUROGENIC SHOCK

Spinal shock is the acute, transient loss of motor, sensory and reflex function below the level of injury. Neurogenic shock is tissue hypoperfusion from disruption of sympathetic control of vascular tone, especially with injuries above T6: hypotension with bradycardia, and the skin may be warm and pink. It has been documented to last up to 4 to 5 weeks (StatPearls 2023). In the multiply injured patient, distributive shock after spinal cord injury may overlap with haemorrhagic shock (ESICM 2026), so treat haemorrhage as the cause until proven otherwise.

3. NICE NG41: IMMOBILISATION, DESTINATION AND IMAGING

Canadian C-spine rule: high risk is age 65 or over, a dangerous mechanism (for example a fall from more than 1 metre or 5 steps, axial load to the head, high-speed or rollover collision, ejection) or paraesthesia in the limbs. A person with a low-risk factor who can actively rotate the neck 45 degrees left and right does not need imaging. Immobilisation in adults: manual in-line stabilisation, then a correctly sized semi-rigid collar unless the airway is compromised or there is a known deformity such as ankylosing spondylitis (keep the spine in its current position); reassess the airway; then scoop stretcher, head blocks and tape, ideally in a vacuum mattress. In uncooperative, agitated or distressed patients, including children, consider letting them find a comfortable position with manual in-line stabilisation. A longboard is for extrication only, never for transport. Destination: suspected spinal cord injury goes to a major trauma centre irrespective of transfer time unless an immediate life-saving intervention is needed, and never directly from scene to a spinal cord injury centre; children with suspected column injury go to a major trauma centre. Adults (16 or over): CT if the Canadian C-spine rule indicates imaging, or if there is strong suspicion of thoracic or lumbosacral injury with abnormal neurology. If a neurological abnormality could be due to cord injury, perform MRI after CT even when CT shows nothing (clarified May 2021). Suspected T1–L3 column injury without neurological signs: X-ray first, then CT if the X-ray is abnormal or there are clinical signs or symptoms of column injury. A new column fracture means imaging the rest of the spine. Children (under 16): MRI if there is strong suspicion of cervical cord or column injury; consider plain X-rays if MRI criteria are not met but suspicion remains after repeated clinical assessment, and discuss them with a consultant radiologist; do not routinely use whole-body CT. The spinal surgeon at the MTC or trauma unit should contact the linked spinal cord injury centre consultant within 4 hours of diagnosis.

4. THE STEROID CONTROVERSY

NASCIS II (NEJM 1990, double-blind RCT): methylprednisolone 30 mg kg−1 bolus then 5.4 mg kg−1 h−1 for 23 h (n=162) vs naloxone (n=154) vs placebo (n=171). At 6 months, patients given methylprednisolone within 8 h had better motor change scores than placebo (16.0 vs 11.2, P=0.03); those treated after 8 h, and those given naloxone, did not differ from placebo. NASCIS III (JAMA 1997, n=499, all within 8 h and all given the bolus) compared 24 h with 48 h of methylprednisolone and with tirilazad. It advised 24 h if started within 3 h and 48 h if started at 3–8 h, but the 48-h regimen started at 3–8 h brought more severe sepsis and severe pneumonia. Guidance diverges. AANS/CNS moved from listing methylprednisolone as an option, with a harm caveat (2002), to a level I recommendation against it (2013). The AO Spine 2017 guideline wording is: “We suggest not offering a 24-hour infusion of high-dose MPSS to adult patients who present after 8 hours with acute SCI”; “We suggest a 24-hour infusion of high-dose MPSS be offered to adult patients within 8 hours of acute SCI as a treatment option”; and “We suggest not offering a 48-hour infusion of high-dose MPSS to adult patients with acute SCI.” All three are weak recommendations, and the motor benefit behind the second was small (3–4 motor points). So AO Spine leaves a weak option within 8 h, whereas the UK says no.

UK position (NICE NG41, 2016): do not use methylprednisolone, nimodipine or naloxone, aimed at neuroprotection, in the acute stage after traumatic spinal cord injury (recommendation 1.7.3). The management of each spinal cord injury should be agreed between spinal surgery and spinal cord injury specialists (1.7.2). nice.org.uk/guidance/ng41

5. TIMING OF DECOMPRESSION AND MAP TARGETS

STASCIS (PLoS One 2012) was a prospective cohort, not an RCT: 313 adults aged 16–80 with cervical cord injury at 6 North American centres, early surgery (under 24 h, n=182, mean 14.2 h) vs late (n=131, mean 48.3 h). Of the 222 followed up at 6 months, 19.8% vs 8.8% improved by 2 or more AIS grades (OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.11 to 5.97; adjusted OR 2.83, 1.10 to 7.28). The AO Spine 2017 guideline made a weak suggestion (low-quality evidence) to offer early surgery regardless of level, and in central cord syndrome. The AO Spine/Praxis 2024 update made it a strong recommendation (moderate-quality evidence): offer surgery within 24 hours, regardless of level, when medically feasible. Its pooled estimates were a risk ratio of 2.76 (95% CI 1.60 to 4.98) for a 2-grade AIS gain at 6 months and 4.50 extra ASIA motor points (95% CI 1.70 to 7.29). It made no recommendation on ultra-early surgery (under 12 h). NG41 does not address operative timing. MAP: the 2013 AANS/CNS guideline recommended maintaining MAP between 85 and 90 mmHg for the first 7 days. The AO Spine/Praxis 2024 haemodynamic guideline, which updated it, suggests augmenting MAP to a lower limit of at least 75–80 mmHg, not actively beyond an upper limit of 90–95 mmHg, for 3–7 days. Both are weak recommendations based on very low-quality evidence. Systolic pressure below 90 mmHg should be avoided or corrected; no recommendation was made on the choice of vasopressor. Older adults and associated injuries: age 65 or over is itself a high-risk factor for cervical injury in NG41, as is age 65 or over with thoracic or lumbosacral pain, and being known to have, or at risk of, osteoporosis is a factor for thoracolumbar injury.
