Lead — FPHC consensus 2025: self-extrication or minimally assisted extrication is now the standard first line for every casualty without contraindications, using U-STEP OUT, and the aim of every plan is the shortest safe entrapment time, not the least possible movement. NFCC adopted this in National Operational Guidance in January 2025. Change this month: check that local SOPs, training and assessment use U-STEP OUT and its contraindications, appoint an extrication buddy at every job, and treat absolute movement minimisation as one factor, not the goal. How strong is the evidence? Mostly UK registry data, volunteer laboratory studies and expert consensus. There are no trials, no study yet links measured spinal movement to harm, and much of the work comes from one research group (EXIT, now IMPACT). The direction is consistent with independent studies from Spain, Germany and the US. Core Revision: the trapped casualty, from entrapment types and U-STEP OUT to rapid versus controlled extrication, where NICE differs, and a glossary of clinical terms.

FOR THE NEXT INCIDENT

GUIDELINE Self-extrication first: for every casualty without contraindications (cannot understand or follow instructions; cannot stand, for example suspected pelvic or leg fracture, impalement, dizziness or confusion). Use U-STEP OUT. (FPHC 2025; NFCC CC 105)

GUIDELINE Time, not millimetres: "A focus on absolute movement minimisation is not a justified single approach" (NFCC). Plan for the shortest safe entrapment.

DX Who needs a rapid extrication: casualties responding only to pain (90% needed critical care) or unresponsive (86%), or with no radial pulse or a compromised airway; alert casualties rarely did (3%). (APEX, derivation only)

OBS Self-extrication is gentlest and fastest: 2.6 mm neck movement and 6.4 s, against 5.2 to 6.2 mm and roughly 54 to 67 s for tool methods in pre-cut cars (6 volunteers).

OBS Collars: a collar reduces neck movement during self-extrication, but fitting one in the car used up the saving (9.5 vs 9.4 mm). Collars for everyone was rejected by UK experts.

QUAL Talk to them: an extrication buddy, explanation, early pain relief, family contact and screening from onlookers were what trapped casualties valued most.

FOR TRAINING AND POLICY

OBS Trapped casualties are sicker: 30-day mortality 8.9% vs 5.0%; serious chest injury 53% vs 35%; spinal cord injury in about 1 in 94 recorded extrications (calculated). (TARN)

OBS Age is not a bar: about 4 in 10 trapped casualties, at every age, had none of the agreed barriers to self-extrication.

OBS Women are trapped more: 15.8% vs 9.4%, with more pelvic injury, which is a U-STEP OUT contraindication.

OBS KEDs cost minutes: 225 s vs 55 s for manual rescue through a car door (117 firefighters) and 247 s vs 80 s from a truck cab (36), with no less neck movement.

GUIDELINE The clock starts at injury: rapid extrication is defined as within 5 minutes for critical casualties (2025 Delphi).

NICE Where guidance differs: NICE NG41 (2016) still excludes casualties with spinal pain or any abnormal neurological symptoms from self-extrication; FPHC and NFCC do not. Agree the plan with clinicians at the vehicle.

Self-Extrication First: FPHC Consensus (43 Experts; All 12 Draft Statements Agreed) Moves UK Extrication From Absolute Movement Minimisation to Time-Focused, Casualty-Centred Rescue

Nutbeam T, et al. Extrication following a motor vehicle collision: a consensus statement on behalf of The Faculty of Pre-hospital Care, Royal College of Surgeons of Edinburgh. Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med 2025;33:3. doi:10.1186/s13049-024-01312-z. PMID: 39762917. Source route: PMC full text (CC BY 4.0) and the FPHC publication PDF. Appraised from full text

What they did. The Faculty of Pre-hospital Care (FPHC) combined a scoping review (7,083 records screened, 170 articles kept) with the EXIT project research. On 15 April 2024, 43 subject matter experts from fire and rescue services, UKRO, NFCC, ambulance services, air ambulances, the College of Policing and other bodies voted anonymously on 12 draft statements; 70% agreement counted as consensus. All 12 reached it. The steering group then wrote 14 final statements, which the FPHC ratified. What it says, in short. Treat every injured casualty as time dependent and plan with the primary aim of cutting entrapment time. Self-extrication or minimally assisted extrication is the standard first line for anyone without contraindications, and non-clinicians (including firefighters) should be empowered to choose and deliver it, using a tool such as U-STEP OUT (figure below). Fully conscious casualties without neurological symptoms do not need manual in-line stabilisation in the vehicle. Hard neurological signs (for example, cannot move the legs) mean rapid extrication with gentle handling, not absolute movement minimisation. Collars reduce neck movement: apply one before extrication only when indicated and consider removing it afterwards. Move the vehicle if that shortens entrapment. Keep observations and in-car treatment to what is critical. Apply a pelvic binder after extrication. Plan for the psychological impact, and run joint FRS and ambulance case reviews. How strongly the panel agreed. Self-extrication was judged appropriate with neck or back pain by 92% (3% no, 5% opted out), with soft neurological signs such as non-dermatomal tingling by 92%, and with central cord signs by 74% (9% no, 17% opted out). Every final statement is graded level III or IV, grade D: it rests on observational, laboratory and expert-opinion evidence, not on trials.

NFCC National Operational Guidance (CC 105, January 2025; Revised April 2025): U-STEP OUT Added to Self-Extrication First, and Crews May Progress to Final Extrication Before Medical Responders Arrive

National Fire Chiefs Council. Control measure: casualty-centred rescue from a mode of transport (initial actions). National Operational Guidance. Change request CC 105 approved 22 January 2025; paragraph revised 24 April 2025. NFCC control measure; NFCC notice of the April revision; NFCC notice of the January change; previous version (PDF). Source route: NFCC website, read in full 10 and 11 November 2026. Appraised from full text

What it tells incident commanders. Build a casualty-centred plan with all agencies "with the primary focus of minimising entrapment time". Handle casualties gently and keep their dignity. "A focus on absolute movement minimisation is not a justified single approach and should be balanced against the speed of access and casualty extrication." Prioritise removing physical entrapment so that a rapid rescue stays possible if the casualty deteriorates. U-STEP OUT contraindications listed by NFCC. The casualty cannot understand or follow instructions; or cannot stand, or is suspected to be unable to stand, for example suspected pelvic fracture, impalement, suspected or confirmed leg fractures, or dizziness or confusion. What CC 105 changed. We compared the current text with the previous version that NFCC published for comparison. The previous version already made self-extrication or minimally assisted extrication the standard first line, with the same contraindications, the extrication buddy and vehicle relocation; we could not establish from NFCC pages when that wording was first introduced. CC 105 added the U-STEP OUT principles and cue card, and says self-extrication should be applied when identified "through a multi-agency risk assessment or by fire and rescue service personnel following the U-STEP OUT principles". It removed the sentence "Medical responders should determine when and how extrication should be performed". It added early resource requests using vehicle eCall or SOS alerts, the statement that initial access for urgent medical help should not be delayed by stabilisation unless the vehicle is unsafe, and that in phase 2 stabilisation "Absolute stabilisation is not necessary". It added one strategic action: "Consider implementing clinical governance arrangements and training following the U-STEP OUT principles". The April 2025 revision says that if immediate medical help is not available and the casualty can be safely supported, crews should take proactive steps and may progress the incident to a safe final extrication before medical responders arrive, if required. Related changes. NFCC changed the hazard and two further control measures (situational awareness, and appropriate access) at the same time, and on 24 April 2025 approved matching changes to Fire Control Guidance for calls about incidents involving a road vehicle (NFCC notice). The fire control text itself sits behind an NFCC login and we have not read it.

Delphi of 60 Rescue and Clinical Experts (2022): Consensus on 91 Statements, Including Self-Extrication as First Line, Fewer KEDs, Longboards for Extrication Only, and No Collars for Everyone

Nutbeam T, et al. A Delphi study of rescue and clinical subject matter experts on the extrication of patients following a motor vehicle collision. Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med 2022;30:41. doi:10.1186/s13049-022-01029-x. PMID: 35725580. Source route: PMC full text (CC BY). Appraised from full text

What they did. Stakeholder organisations nominated 60 rescue and clinical experts, who rated statements over three online rounds (60 in round 1, 53 in round 2, 49 in round 3); the study follows the CREDES reporting standard for Delphi studies. What was agreed (91 statements: 89 agreed, 2 rejected; 33 without consensus). Self-extrication or minimally assisted extrication should be the standard first line for trapped casualties "who are conscious and likely to be able to stand with assistance". Contraindications: cannot understand or follow instructions; injuries or baseline function that prevent standing on at least one leg (for example unstable pelvic fracture, impalement, bilateral leg fracture). Kendrick Extrication Devices (KED) prolong extrication and their use should be minimised. Longboards are an extrication device, not for carrying the casualty beyond the extrication phase. Call handlers should ask casualties to self-extricate if able and the scene is safe, and advise bystanders not to move casualties with a reduced conscious level unless there is an immediate threat to life. The statement "Cervical collars should be used where available on all patients as a movement minimisation tool" was rejected; collars should be loosened or removed after extrication as clinical assessment dictates.

Physically Trapped Casualties, Delphi of 66 Experts (2025): 104 Principles Agreed, Including "Rapid Extrication Within 5 Minutes" for Critical Casualties and a Clock That Starts at the Time of Injury

Nutbeam T, et al. Optimising the care of the trapped patient following a motor vehicle collision: a UK-based Delphi consensus study. Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med 2025;33:137. doi:10.1186/s13049-025-01449-5. PMID: 40790207. Source route: PMC full text (CC BY). Appraised from full text

What they did. 66 experts (45 clinical, 68%; 17 fire and rescue, 26%; the rest both) rated statements over three rounds from January to March 2025; 70% agreement counted as consensus. This study is about the casualty who is physically trapped and cannot self-extricate. Key agreed principles. Entrapment includes physical entrapment by the vehicle and functional entrapment from pain, injury or physiological impairment. Rapid extrication is "within 5 min" for critically ill or deteriorating casualties (for example hypovolaemia or suspected severe head injury); controlled extrication is structured, for non-critical casualties. Time-sensitive decisions should assume "the clock starts at the time of injury, not at the arrival of rescuers or clinicians". Clinical assessment in the vehicle is often unreliable for ruling out time-critical conditions, so it should focus only on immediately life-threatening problems such as catastrophic haemorrhage or airway obstruction; a full secondary survey waits until after extrication. Non-medical first responders should be trained to recognise time-critical conditions and start an appropriate extrication strategy before the medical team arrives. Extrication itself is a medical intervention that should prompt reassessment.

What they did. The same expert panel and methods as the previous item, asked which injuries are time-critical (immediate threat to life) or time-dependent (risk grows with delay), and which interventions matter most during entrapment. Time-critical: catastrophic haemorrhage; a completely or nearly completely obstructed airway; airway burns; penetrating chest injury with respiratory compromise or major bleeding; pelvic fracture with major haemorrhage; non-compressible haemorrhage; severe head injury with airway compromise or signs of herniation. NAEMSP Review (2025, US): 115 Papers, 55 Showing Harms of Spinal Immobilisation and None Showing Definitive Benefit; Boards and Vacuum Splints Only for Active Extrication

Millin MG, et al. Prehospital trauma compendium: prehospital management of spinal cord injuries. A NAEMSP comprehensive review and analysis of the literature. What they did. A structured review for the US National Association of EMS Physicians searched four databases back to 1900 and screened 3,944 papers; 115 were included. What it found. Two case series proposed movement after injury as the cause of delayed neurological injury; eight retrospective studies linked low blood pressure and poor perfusion with worsening spinal cord injury. 55 studies showed harms of immobilisation and none showed a definitive benefit. The authors conclude that efforts to reduce cervical collar use should be considered, and that backboards and full body vacuum splints should be limited to the time of active extrication.

Trapped Casualties in the UK Trauma Registry (63,625 Patients): Higher 30-Day Mortality (8.9% vs 5.0%) and More Chest Injury, While Trapped Casualties With a Spinal Cord Injury Were About 1 in 100 Recorded Extrications

Nutbeam T, et al. A comparison of the demographics, injury patterns and outcome data for patients injured in motor vehicle collisions who are trapped compared to those patients who are not trapped. Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med 2021;29:17. doi:10.1186/s13049-020-00818-6. PMID: 33446210. Source route: PMC full text (CC BY). Appraised from full text

What they did. TARN (the national trauma registry for England and Wales) data for vehicle collision patients, 2012 to 2018: 63,625 with a recorded trapped status, of whom 6,983 (11.0%) were trapped. "Trapped" means the ambulance record says the patient "needed to be cut free". These were compared with government-published FRS figures for the same years (43,633 extrications). What it found. 30-day mortality was 8.94% in trapped vs 4.95% in not-trapped patients (odds ratio 1.88, 95% CI 1.72 to 2.06; unadjusted). Trapped patients were more badly injured (median injury severity score 18 vs 13) and more often had serious chest injury (53.0% vs 34.7%). They also more often needed time-critical treatment: tranexamic acid 41.1% vs 18.4%, blood transfusion 15.8% vs 6.0%, intubation 22.2% (calculated from 1,547 of 6,983) vs 12.4%. Spinal cord injury was present in 464 trapped patients (6.6%), and half of them (232) also had other significant injuries. Against the 43,633 FRS extrications, trapped TARN patients with a cord injury were about 1 in 94 (calculated); the 232 without other significant injuries were 0.5%.

Core Revision — The Trapped Casualty

Types of entrapment, U-STEP OUT and its contraindications, rapid versus controlled extrication, collars and boards, where UK guidance differs, and a glossary

Goal: explain to a crew why the plan starts with self-extrication, when it must not, when a rapid extrication is needed, and where the clinical guidance they may hear on scene differs.

1. TYPES OF ENTRAPMENT

Physical entrapment: the vehicle structure holds the casualty (for example, dash or footwell intrusion). Functional or medical entrapment: nothing holds them, but pain, injury or illness stops them getting out unaided (2025 Delphi wording: "Entrapment encompasses both physical entrapment by the vehicle structure and functional entrapment due to pain, injuries, or physiological impairment"). In the UK trauma registry, "trapped" means "needed to be cut free" and cannot separate the two.

2. U-STEP OUT

Start with "Stop: does this casualty need a snatch rescue?" Then: Understand (check they understand: wind down the window or give a thumbs up); Support (reassure, explain you will keep them safe, use language they understand); Try moving (ask them to move both legs; if they cannot, they will not be able to self-extricate); Egress (door fully open, obstructions clear); Plan (where will they go: chair or trolley, close to the vehicle?); OUT (ask them to step out, support if needed, do not drag or lift). Source: EXIT cue card, Figure 1. Contraindications (NFCC CC 105): cannot understand or follow instructions; cannot stand, or suspected unable to stand, for example suspected pelvic fracture, impalement, suspected or confirmed leg fractures, dizziness or confusion. The 2022 Delphi phrased the standing test as "standing on at least one leg".

3. RAPID OR CONTROLLED?

Rapid extrication: within 5 minutes, for critically ill or deteriorating casualties (2025 Delphi definition). Controlled extrication: structured, for non-critical casualties, still aiming for the shortest safe time. FPHC: hard neurological signs (for example cannot move the legs) mean rapid extrication with gentle handling. APEX: a casualty responding only to pain or unresponsive, or with no radial pulse or no clear airway, almost always needed critical care after extrication. Time-critical conditions (2025 Delphi) include catastrophic haemorrhage, an obstructed airway, airway burns and pelvic fracture with major bleeding. The decision to go rapid can be made at any stage (NFCC).

4. COLLARS, BOARDS AND DEVICES

FPHC 2025: collars reduce neck movement, so apply one before extrication only when indicated and consider removing it afterwards; no manual in-line stabilisation in the vehicle for fully conscious casualties without neurology; pelvic binder after extrication. NICE NG41 and the 2022 Delphi: a longboard is an extrication device only, not for transport. 2022 Delphi: KEDs prolong extrication and should be minimised.
